Example & Explanation

Understand the Physics behind the walking experiment.

Worked Example

Walking on a normal road

Suppose a person pushes backward on the ground with a force of 60 N, while the ground can provide 50 N of frictional grip.

Push force = 60 N
Available friction = 50 N
Difference ≈ 60 − 50
= 10 N
What You Observe

The person moves forward

When the foot pushes backward against the ground, friction between the shoe and the ground provides the grip required for motion.

Why It Happens

Friction is useful here

Walking would be very difficult without friction. Your foot must interact with the ground to produce forward motion. If the surface becomes very slippery, the available friction becomes too small and the foot starts slipping.

Real-World Connection

Walking on ice

Ice provides much less friction than a rough road. That is why people take shorter, slower steps on ice. Sports shoes also use patterned soles to improve grip.

Remember: Friction is not always an unwanted force. In walking, braking and vehicle movement, friction is essential for controlling motion.

Example & Explanation

See how force and mass control acceleration.

Worked Example

Kicking a football

Suppose the player applies a force of 30 N to a 0.5 kg football.

Force, F = 30 N
Mass, m = 0.5 kg

a = F / m
a = 30 / 0.5
a = 60 m/s²
What You Observe

The ball accelerates rapidly

When kick force increases, the football moves more strongly. If the same force acts on a heavier object, acceleration becomes smaller.

Why It Happens

Newton's Second Law

Acceleration depends directly on force and inversely on mass. This means more force gives greater acceleration, while more mass makes an object harder to accelerate.

Real-World Connection

Football vs heavy medicine ball

If you kick a football and a much heavier ball with approximately the same force, the lighter football accelerates much more easily.

Remember: For the same mass, more force means more acceleration. For the same force, more mass means less acceleration.

Example & Explanation

Understand why a rocket can move upward.

Worked Example

Rocket exhaust interaction

Suppose the rocket engine pushes exhaust gases downward with a force of 70 N.

Force on gases = 70 N downward

Reaction force on rocket
= 70 N upward
What You Observe

The rocket moves upward

Exhaust gases are expelled downward. At the same time, the gases exert an opposite force on the rocket.

Why It Happens

Newton's Third Law

Forces arise as interactions between two objects. The rocket pushes the gases, and the gases push the rocket with an equal-magnitude force in the opposite direction.

Real-World Connection

Rockets work even in space

A rocket does not need air to push against. It carries propellant and accelerates exhaust mass backward, producing the corresponding interaction force on the rocket.

Remember: Action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but they act on different objects.
